B1: Identification of Algorithms

To address the problem of predicting urban air quality–related health risks, several artificial intelligence algorithms were evaluated based on their ability to model environmental and health data. After reviewing multiple approaches, I identified three algorithms as suitable for this task: Linear Regression, Random Forest Regression, and Artificial Neural Networks.

Linear Regression is a supervised learning algorithm commonly used for predicting continuous outcomes. It establishes a linear relationship between independent variables such as pollution levels, weather conditions and a dependent variable, such as a health risk score. While this approach is easy to interpret and efficient, it assumes linear relationships that may not fully capture the complexity of environmental data (GeeksforGeeks, n.d.).

Random Forest Regression is an ensemble learning algorithm that combines multiple decision trees to produce more accurate and stable predictions. Each tree learns different patterns in the data, and the final prediction is generated by averaging the results. This approach is particularly effective when working with non-linear relationships and interacting variables, which are common in air quality and health datasets (GeeksforGeeks, n.d.).

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) consist of interconnected layers of neurons designed to learn complex patterns in data. ANNs are capable of modeling highly non-linear relationships and are often used in large-scale predictive tasks. However, they typically require more data, computational resources, and careful tuning to perform effectively (GeeksforGeeks, n.d.).

B2: Selected Algorithm

Selected Algorithm: Random Forest Regression
B2a. Relationship to the Optimization Problem

The objective of this project is to predict health risk scores based on urban air quality and weather conditions. Random Forest Regression directly supports this goal by learning complex relationships between multiple environmental variables without assuming linearity. The Random Forest Regression algorithm directly supports the objective of this project by predicting health risk scores based on multiple environmental inputs, including air pollution metrics (PM2.5, NO2, and CO2) and weather conditions such as temperature and humidity. In the context of the DQN1 case study, these variables represent real world factors that influence respiratory and cardiovascular health in urban populations (Western Governors University, n.d.).

By training on historical air quality and health data, the Random Forest model learns how combinations of pollution levels and weather conditions correspond to increased or decreased health risks. This allows the model to generate accurate health risk predictions for different environmental scenarios, which can be used by public health officials to identify high risk conditions and take preventive action. Unlike simpler models, Random Forest captures non-linear relationships that commonly occur in environmental systems, making it well suited for assessing complex urban air quality impacts on public health.

B2b: Strengths of the Selected Algorithm

One key strength of Random Forest Regression I noticed is its ability to handle non-linear relationships between input variables and outcomes. Air quality and health risks are influenced by multiple interacting factors, and Random Forest models are effective at capturing these interactions.

Another strength is its robustness against overfitting. Because predictions are averaged across many decision trees, the model is less sensitive to noise in the dataset and generally performs well on unseen data (GeeksforGeeks, n.d.).

B2c: Potential Limitations of the Selected Algorithm

Despite its advantages, Random Forest Regression has some limitations. One limitation is reduced interpretability compared to simpler models like Linear Regression.

Additionally, Random Forest models require more computational resources than simpler algorithms. Training many decision trees can increase processing time and memory usage, particularly for large datasets.


D1: Evaluation Metrics I Used

I selected two evaluation metrics to assess the performance of the Random Forest model:

Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) measures the average magnitude of prediction errors and penalizes larger errors more heavily. It provides insight into how closely the model’s predictions align with actual health risk values.

Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) expresses prediction error as a percentage, making it easier to interpret model accuracy in relative terms.

D2: Implementing the Eval Metrics

Both RMSE and MAPE were implemented using the scikit-learn metrics library in Python. These metrics were calculated by comparing the model’s predicted health risk scores with the actual values from the test dataset.

D3: Analysis of Metrics

The Random Forest model demonstrated strong predictive performance based on the selected evaluation metrics. The RMSE value of 0.2696 indicates that the model’s predictions closely align with observed health risk scores, with relatively small average errors.

In addition, the MAPE value of approximately 2.09% suggests that the model is highly accurate, with predictions deviating only slightly from actual values on average. Together, these results indicate that the model effectively captures the relationship between air quality indicators, weather conditions, and associated health risks.

Overall, my evaluation results confirm that Random Forest Regression is an effective approach for predicting health risk outcomes in urban environments based on environmental data.

D4: Improvement 

While the model performed well, there are always opportunities for improvement. One potential improvment could be simply using a larger dataset or even adding more features. Including real-time data sources and performing hyperparameter tuning could also further improve accuracy.
